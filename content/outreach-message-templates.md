# 10 Outreach Message Templates That Get Replies

Outreach fails for predictable reasons: it is too long, too generic, too focused on the sender, or it asks for too much too soon. The templates below are built to avoid all four traps. Each one is original, written in a professional tone, and designed to be adapted — not copied verbatim and blasted to a thousand people.

A few ground rules before the templates. First, every template assumes you have done basic research on the recipient — their role, their company, one real observation. Without that, no template saves you. Second, keep every message under 150 words unless the context genuinely demands more; respect is shown through brevity. Third, one clear call to action per message. And fourth: never misrepresent who you are, what you offer, or your relationship to the recipient. Deceptive outreach burns the exact trust you are trying to build.

Each template includes a **When to use it** note and **one personalization tip**. Adapt the bracketed placeholders to your situation.

## 1. The Cold Email Opener

**When to use it:** First contact with a prospect who matches your ideal customer profile but has no prior relationship with you. Best for B2B services where you can name a specific, observable reason for reaching out.

> Subject: Quick thought on [Company]'s [specific area, e.g., onboarding flow]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> I noticed [Company] recently [specific, verifiable observation — e.g., launched a new pricing page / expanded into the German market / hired three support reps]. Congratulations — that's a meaningful step.
>
> One thing companies at this stage often run into is [specific problem tied to your expertise]. We help [type of companies] solve it by [one-line description of your approach, no jargon].
>
> Would it be useful if I sent over a short breakdown of how we'd approach this for [Company]? No pitch, just the thinking.
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** The observation in the first line must be something you genuinely found — a product update, a job posting, a podcast appearance. If you cannot find one real observation, the prospect is not researched enough to contact yet.

## 2. The Follow-Up Bump

**When to use it:** Three to five business days after an unanswered opener. This is a nudge, not a new pitch — its only job is to resurface the first message.

> Subject: Re: Quick thought on [Company]'s [specific area]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> Floating this back to the top of your inbox in case it got buried.
>
> The short version: I put together a few ideas on [the specific topic from your first email] for [Company]. Happy to send them over if useful — or if the timing's off, just say so and I won't follow up again.
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Reference something timely if you can — "I saw your team just announced [X], which makes this more relevant" — but only if it is true. A bump with a genuine new reason to talk beats a bump that just says "bumping this."

## 3. The Value-First LinkedIn-Style Note

**When to use it:** When connecting on LinkedIn or a similar platform before any pitch. The goal is a conversation, not a conversion — you are earning the right to follow up later.

> Hi [First Name], I came across your [post/article/comment] on [topic] and your point about [specific point they made] resonated — it's something we're seeing with [type of companies] as well.
>
> I wrote a short piece on [related topic] recently that takes a slightly different angle. Happy to share it if you're interested — no strings attached.
>
> Either way, glad to connect with people thinking seriously about [topic].
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Quote or paraphrase their actual words back to them. "Your point about onboarding drop-off after day 14" proves you read it; "great post about SaaS" proves you did not.

## 4. The Referral Ask

**When to use it:** After you have delivered real value to a client or contact — a completed project, a helpful introduction, a problem solved. Never ask for referrals from people you have not helped yet.

> Subject: A quick favor, [First Name]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> Really glad [specific outcome — e.g., the new follow-up system is saving your team those Monday mornings]. It's been great working with you.
>
> Quick question: do you know one or two [role, e.g., agency owners / ops managers] who might be dealing with [the problem you solved]? I'm looking to help more teams with exactly this, and a warm introduction from you would mean a lot.
>
> No pressure at all — and I'm happy to return the favor anytime.
>
> Thanks,
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Make it easy by suggesting the profile, not asking them to think of names from scratch. "Anyone in your network running a 10-to-30-person agency" is answerable; "do you know anyone" is homework.

## 5. The Breakup Email

**When to use it:** Your final follow-up after two or three unanswered touches. Its purpose is to either provoke a response or close the loop cleanly so you stop spending attention on a dead thread.

> Subject: Should I close the loop, [First Name]?
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> I've reached out a couple of times about [topic] and haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right and stop following up.
>
> If things change — or if someone else on your team owns [area] — I'm easy to find. Wishing [Company] well with [specific initiative you noticed].
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** The final line referencing their initiative is what separates a professional close from a guilt trip. It shows you were paying attention to them, not just your pipeline.

## 6. The Post-Demo Follow-Up

**When to use it:** Within 24 hours of a demo or discovery call. Speed matters here — it signals how you run your business.

> Subject: Next steps from today's call, [First Name]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> Thanks for the time today — especially the context on [specific thing they shared, e.g., the Q1 expansion plans / the current manual reporting process]. That helped me understand where [your solution] would fit.
>
> As discussed, here's what happens next:
>
> - [Step 1, e.g., I'll send the proposal with the two package options by Thursday]
> - [Step 2, e.g., You'll loop in Priya from ops for the technical review]
> - [Step 3, e.g., We'll reconnect next Tuesday to walk through questions]
>
> I've attached [anything promised]. Let me know if I missed anything.
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Include one detail they mentioned that was not strictly business — within professional bounds. It proves you listened to the person, not just the requirements.

## 7. The Cold-Lead Revival

**When to use it:** Re-engaging a prospect who went quiet 3+ months ago, or an old lead from your database. The angle is "something changed," not "just checking in."

> Subject: Something changed at [Company] — worth a second look?
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> We spoke back in [month] about [topic], and the timing wasn't right then. Since we last talked, [genuine development on your side — e.g., we've launched a done-for-you version that cuts setup from weeks to days / we've added support for the CRM your team uses].
>
> Given [Company]'s [recent observable development on their side], it might be worth a fresh 15-minute look. If not, no worries — I'll leave you be.
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** The revival needs a real reason — something new on your side, something new on theirs, or a genuinely new angle. "Just circling back" after six months of silence is not a reason; it is an admission you have nothing new to say.

## 8. The Event Follow-Up

**When to use it:** Within 48 hours of meeting someone at a conference, webinar, or networking event. Reference the actual conversation or you are just another business card.

> Subject: Good meeting you at [Event], [First Name]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> Enjoyed our chat at [Event] — particularly your take on [specific topic you discussed]. It's rare to meet someone thinking that carefully about [area].
>
> You mentioned [specific challenge or interest they raised]. I'd like to send over [specific useful thing — an article, an introduction, a quick teardown]. Would that be helpful?
>
> Hope the rest of the event treated you well.
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Write down one detail from the conversation on your phone before you leave the event. "Your take on attribution" is forgettable; "your point that last-click attribution punishes the content team" is a message worth replying to.

## 9. The Testimonial Request

**When to use it:** After a successful engagement with a happy client — ideally right after a win or a positive milestone, when goodwill is highest.

> Subject: Would you be open to a short testimonial, [First Name]?
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> Working with you on [project] has been a highlight — especially [specific result, e.g., getting the follow-up system live before your launch / cutting reporting time to zero].
>
> Would you be open to sharing a few sentences about the experience? It would help other [their peer group] understand what working together looks like. I'm happy to draft something from our results for you to edit — most people find that easier than starting from a blank page.
>
> Either way, thank you for the collaboration.
>
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Offering to draft it is the key move — it removes the blank-page problem and lets them correct rather than create. Always let them edit freely; a testimonial they genuinely approve beats polished words they do not recognize.

## 10. The Partnership Pitch

**When to use it:** Approaching a complementary business — not a competitor, not a prospect — where a partnership would serve both audiences. Lead with what is in it for them.

> Subject: Partnership idea for [Their Company]
>
> Hi [First Name],
>
> I've been following [Their Company]'s work with [their audience, e.g., early-stage SaaS founders], and there's a natural overlap with what we do for [your audience].
>
> Here's the idea: [one or two sentences describing the partnership concretely — e.g., a co-hosted workshop on automation for agencies, with you covering strategy and us covering implementation / a referral arrangement where we send CRM-migration clients your way].
>
> I think your audience would get real value from [the specific benefit], and we'd be glad to [what you contribute — promote it, provide the content, handle the tech].
>
> Open to a 20-minute call to explore it?
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]

**Personalization tip:** Name the concrete format in the first email. "Let's partner somehow" gets ignored; "a co-hosted workshop, you cover X, we cover Y" gives them something to react to. Specificity is what turns a vague idea into a yes-or-no decision.

## How to Use These Without Sounding Like Everyone Else

Templates are starting points, and starting points all sound alike if you never move past them. Three habits keep your outreach human:

- **Rewrite the opening and closing in your own voice.** The middle structure can stay; the wrapper should sound like you. Read it aloud — if you would never say it, do not send it.
- **Send fewer, better messages.** Twenty deeply personalized emails will outperform two hundred mail-merged ones in reply rate, meeting rate, and your own reputation. Volume is not a strategy; it is a confession that the message was not good enough.
- **Track what earns replies, not just opens.** Keep a simple log: template used, personalization angle, reply or not, and why you think it worked. After fifty sends you will have your own data on what resonates with your audience — which is worth more than any template collection, including this one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many follow-ups should I send before stopping?

Two to three touches after the opener is the professional standard for cold outreach — an opener, a bump, and a breakup. More than that without any engagement signals persistence turning into pestering. For warm conversations (post-demo, referrals), follow the thread naturally rather than counting touches.

### Do these templates work for LinkedIn messages too?

Mostly yes, with compression. LinkedIn messages and connection notes have tighter practical limits on attention — cut any template roughly in half for LinkedIn, keep the personalization line, and keep the call to action to one low-friction ask. The value-first note (template 3) was written for that environment natively.

### Should I use automation to send outreach at scale?

Automation can handle the mechanics — sequencing, reminders, logging replies — but the personalization must be real. Tools that mail-merge first names into generic templates produce the spam everyone deletes. If you automate outreach, automate the workflow around genuinely personalized messages, not the personalization itself. And always comply with anti-spam laws in your jurisdiction (such as CAN-SPAM in the US or GDPR/PECR in Europe), including a working opt-out.

### What is the biggest mistake people make with cold email?

Leading with themselves. "I'm the founder of X, we do Y, we're the leading Z" — the recipient does not care yet. Every template above leads with the recipient: something you noticed, something they said, something they need. Earn attention with relevance before you spend it on your pitch.

### How do I personalize at scale without spending an hour per prospect?

Build a research checklist with five quick checks: recent company news, a job posting, a podcast or post, a product change, a mutual connection. Spend five minutes per prospect on two of the five. That is enough for one genuine observation, which is all any template needs. Batch the research separately from the writing — context-switching between the two is what makes it slow.

### Is the breakup email manipulative?

It can be, if written as a guilt trip ("I guess you're not interested in growing your business"). Written as above — a clean, respectful close with a door left open — it is simply honest. You are telling the truth: you are going to stop following up. Prospects respect clarity, and a meaningful share of replies to breakup emails are genuine.

## Conclusion

Good outreach is not a copywriting trick. It is relevance, brevity, and respect, repeated consistently. The ten templates above give you the structure; the personalization tip under each one gives you the ingredient that makes the structure work.

Start with one template and one small batch. Personalize properly, track replies, and refine based on your own results. Within a few weeks you will have something better than any template collection: a voice and a system that are yours — and a reply rate to prove it.
